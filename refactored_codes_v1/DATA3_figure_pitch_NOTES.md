# DATA3 figure-pitch — speaker notes

_Running example throughout: squeezing salty water through a coffee filter (Lp = water passes easily, σ = blocks salt by osmosis, B = salt leaks by diffusion)._


## Title slide

OPENING (30 s). We fit three transport parameters to single-salt NF270 diafiltration data and ask which are really identifiable.

Punchline of the whole talk: Lp (water permeability) and B (salt permeability) are well determined; σ (salt rejection) is NOT — it is forced to its bound; and the concentration-dependence of B cannot be located from a single salt.

Running picture — squeezing salty water through a coffee filter:
  • Lp = how EASILY water passes (bigger Lp → more water per push)
  • σ  = how well the filter BLOCKS salt by osmosis (σ=1 blocks all, σ=0 lets salt ride along)
  • B  = how much salt LEAKS through by diffusion
  • ΔP = how hard you push;  Δπ = the osmotic 'back-push' from the salt difference


## Slide 2 — Figure 1

FIT CHECK — diluting NaCl. Does the model reproduce the experiment?

What it shows: lines = model, symbols = measurements. LEFT = retentate mass vs time; RIGHT = concentration vs time (TEAL = retentate, RED = permeate; teal diamonds = retentate ICP at start & end).

Simple steps: (1) choose Lp, σ, B; (2) march the equations forward in time; (3) overlay on the data. The inset box reports the best-fit values.

Coffee-filter example: as water is pushed out, the cup (retentate) gets saltier and lighter while the drips (permeate) carry a little salt.

Core model (3 fitted parameters Lp, σ, B):
  Water flux   Jw = Lp·(ΔP − σ·Δπ)          [net push = applied − osmotic back-push]
  Osmotic      Δπ = i·R·T·(cF − cP)         [i = ions/molecule: NaCl 2, CaCl₂ 3, LaCl₃ 4]
  Salt flux    Js = B·(cF − cP) + (1−σ)·Jw·c̄  [diffusion term + convection term]
  Mass balance dM/dt = −ρ·A·Jw              [retentate lightens as permeate leaves]

This run: Lp = 8.98, B = 13.88 µm/s, σ = 1 (σ forced to its upper bound).

## Slide 6 — Figure 5

IDENTIFIABILITY MAP — diluting NaCl. Can the data tell σ and B apart?

What it shows: with Lp FIXED at its identified value (Lp = 8.98), we map the fit error over σ (x-axis) and B (y-axis). Three panels = the three things we measure (mass, permeate, retentate). Red triangle = best fit.

Simple steps: (1) fix Lp; (2) try every (σ, B) on a grid; (3) simulate, score the error, color the map (blue = good fit, red = bad). Titles say mass/permeate/retentate; the color is log₁₀ of the weighted error (WSSE).

How to read it: the contours run nearly FLAT along the σ direction → moving σ barely changes the error → σ is NOT identifiable and is forced to its upper bound (σ = 1). The error DOES change with B → B is well determined.

Coffee-filter example: the filter blocks salt so completely that you can't measure exactly how well — σ just pegs at 'fully blocking.'

Math: WSSE = Σ[(model − data)/noise]², plotted as log₁₀. A long flat valley = many equally-good answers = not identifiable; a tight bowl = identifiable.

## Slide 10 — Figure 9

WHY B DEPENDS ON CONCENTRATION (diagnostic 1). 

What it shows: LEFT — regression error vs Péclet number for each salt; RIGHT — simulated salt flux Js vs concentration with a convection–diffusion fit overlaid.

Math: Péclet number Pe = Jw·L/Dm = (transport by water being pushed through) ÷ (transport by diffusion). L = membrane thickness, Dm = diffusivity. Pe > 1 means convection wins; Pe < 1 means diffusion wins.

Simple steps: forward-simulate Jw and the concentrations, then regress how salt partitions vs Pe.

How to read it: the three concentrating salts minimize their error at HIGH Pe (convection-dominated → B varies with c); diluting NaCl is the counter-example at LOW Pe (diffusion → B roughly constant).

Coffee-filter example: a slow drip (diffusion) and a firehose (convection) through the same filter behave differently — that's why the salt permeability isn't a single fixed number.


## Slide 11 — Figure 10

WHY B DEPENDS ON CONCENTRATION (diagnostic 2 — the one-line version).

What it shows: one number per salt — its operating Péclet — on a log axis. Blue zone = diffusion (Pe < 1), orange zone = convection (Pe > 1), line at Pe = 1.

Punchline: the SAME membrane spans BOTH regimes. Diluting NaCl sits deep in diffusion (Pe ≈ 0.001); the three concentrating salts sit in convection (Pe ≈ 36–77). That is the mechanistic reason the salt permeability is concentration-dependent for the concentrating runs.

Math reminder: Pe = Jw·L/Dm. Crossing Pe = 1 is the switch from diffusion- to convection-controlled transport.


## Slide 12 — Figure 11

THE SHAPE OF B(c) — and why our fit looks flat.

Math: the mechanistic Donnan partition is
   B(c) = Jw·P0·(−X + √(X² + 4k²c²)) / (2c).
Two knobs: the PLATEAU height P0·k (reached at high salt) and the KNEE location c_knee = X/(2k) (where the curve bends). At low c it rises ~linearly from zero; at high c it flattens to the plateau.

Simple steps: rescale every salt onto ONE universal master curve using u = c/c_knee, then mark where each salt's MEASURED window lands on it.

How to read it: every measured window sits far out on the FLAT plateau (u ≈ 10³–10⁵). That's because the fit pushes the charge term X to its lower bound, so the knee falls ~1000× below where we actually measure.

Punchline: the saturating shape is the correct physics, but a single salt only samples the flat TOP of it — so we cannot locate the knee (the curvature) from one salt at a time. Honest framing: 'one salt can't see it,' NOT 'the membrane is flat.'

Coffee-filter example: we're standing on the flat summit far past the slope — we know we're high up, but can't see where the hill rose.


## Slide 13 — Figure 12

WHICH B(c) FORM TO FIT — LaCl₃.

What it shows: the mechanistic Donnan curve (solid black) vs simple polynomial 'Taylor' fits and a saturating-exp fit, INSIDE the measured window (left) and EXTRAPOLATED beyond it (right).

Math: a Taylor series B(c) ≈ β₀ + β₁c + β₂c² is just a local polynomial approximation — accurate near the data, unreliable far from it.

How to read it: inside the window all forms agree (the Donnan reference is on its flat plateau ≈ 0.3 µm/s, so a constant already captures it). OUTSIDE the window the polynomials blow up unphysically (cubic turns negative, quadratic curls up) while Donnan stays bounded.

Punchline: use a low-order Taylor INSIDE the calibration range — it's identifiable and sufficient — but do NOT extrapolate it; for transfer across conditions use the mechanistic form.

Coffee-filter example: a straight-line guess between two known points works between them but goes crazy outside them.

## Slide 16 — Figure 15

THE FULL PICTURE OVER TIME (CaCl₂).

What it shows: three panels vs time. A — interface concentration c_in,f vs bulk c_h (their GAP is concentration polarization, salt piling up at the membrane face). B — water flux Jw: model (blue) + MEASURED (black dotted). C — salt flux Js.

Simple steps: forward-simulate at the fitted parameters; get the measured water flux from how fast permeate mass is collected in each vial.

Math: measured Jw = (dm/dt)/(ρ·A) — collection rate ÷ (density × membrane area); model Jw = Lp·(ΔP − σ·Δπ).

How to read it: as the cell concentrates, both concentrations rise → osmotic back-push grows → water flux Jw declines (and the model tracks the measured dotted line) → salt flux Js climbs. All consistent with concentration-dependent transport.

Coffee-filter example: the saltier the cup gets, the harder osmosis pushes back, so the drip slows down over time.
